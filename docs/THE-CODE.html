The code

5,343 bytes of code and 11,041 of data. The main thread has been stuck in a jr $ since boot, so everything that follows happens inside the VDP interrupt, fifty times a second.

The interrupt

0x4034 does four things in this order: acknowledges the VDP — which is what lowers the signal — steps the sound player, checks the latch at 0xE005 and, if it is free, reads the controls and runs one step of the game.

The latch matters. Just before entering the game step, the routine does ei: it opens interrupts while it works. If the step overruns a frame — and the physics of seven balls does overrun — the next interrupt comes in, sees the latch closed and leaves without doing anything but servicing the sound. The music does not stutter even when the game is running tight.

Sixteen states and a table with no pointer

The game step (0x4337) increments the frame counter and dispatches on 0xE000, the state, which runs from 0 to 15.

The dispatcher (0x40A0) is not given the table's address. It does pop hl to take its own return address and use it as the base, so the table of sixteen words sits immediately behind the call that invoked it, and control never returns there. It ends in jp (hl).

There are three such tables in the cartridge: the sixteen states behind the call at 0x434D, the three tally phases behind the one at 0x5ACA, and the four demonstration steps behind the one at 0x5EB6.

The states do what you would expect: 0 to 7 are the title screen and menu, 8 confirms the selection, 9 racks a ball, 10 sets the table, 11 is the shot, 12 decides it, 13 changes player, 14 waits after GAME OVER and 15 celebrates a cleared table. State 3 is a dead step: its table entry points at the instruction that only increments the state.

A ball's record

The table is eight sixteen-byte records from 0xE100:

bytewhat it is
+00xFF while the ball is still on the cloth
+1the energy it has left; at zero, it has stopped
+2the angle it is travelling at
+3 and +4Y in 16 bits, with the integer part in +4
+5 and +6X in 16 bits, with the integer part in +6
+7 and +8the Y velocity, signed
+9 and +10the X velocity
+11 and +12sprite pattern number and colour
+13 and +14the friction, split by component
+15which ball it last collided with

The position is fixed point 8.8, and the integer byte lands exactly where the sprite dump will read it: moving a ball is adding two 16-bit words to two others, and the sprite finds out by itself.

The four bytes the VDP needs — +4, +6, +11 and +12 — are dumped by 0x466D in one go through the data port, riding the VDP's auto-increment. And the order of the two groups of four balls alternates with bit 0 of the frame counter, the usual trick against the TMS9918's sprites-per-line limit.

The physics, three routines

Moving (0x4E6B) adds the velocity to the position and checks whether the ball has left: the table runs from X=0x14 to X=0xD8 and from Y=0x3E to Y=0xA1. If it has left where there is a cushion, it reflects it — position minus twice the overshoot — and negates that velocity component. If it has left where there is a pocket, it goes to the routine that sinks it.

Colliding (0x4F25) walks the other six balls with a cheap sieve: if the two coordinates differ by less than twelve pixels, contact is possible. Then 0x4F63 measures the real distance, with the two squares summed and compared against 0x9000, which at that scale is the same twelve pixels.

Friction (0x53DD) comes in one frame in every eight: it takes 3 off the energy and adds to each velocity component the friction held in +13 and +14, always with the opposite sign to the motion. Since those two bytes are the unit vector components scaled to 24/256, the ball slows down along its own line and does not veer as it stops.

How a collision is shared out

If both balls are rolling, the cartridge swaps their velocities and that is that (0x5192): the head-on collision between equal masses, solved without a single multiplication.

If one was at rest, then there is a calculation. It measures the angle of the line joining the two centres, compares it with the angle the ball was travelling at, and that difference — saturated at 0x3C, nearly a right angle — goes into the cosine table: the part of the motion falling along the line of centres is handed over, the perpendicular part stays. The striking ball leaves along the perpendicular, and for that the two components are crossed as they are written, which is turning the vector ninety degrees without touching it.

Directions with no sines or cosines

A direction is not kept as an angle, but as a slope within an octant plus two mirror bits. The routine at 0x5430 takes the two velocity components, keeps their absolute values, notes in 0xE1A2 which way each was heading, divides both by 32 and compares: the larger becomes the divisor and the smaller the dividend, so the quotient always lands between 0 and 255 and tables of 256 entries are enough.

There are four, all checked against their formula with a maximum error of 1:

tablewhat it holds
0x539C65 values of 255·cos, for sharing out the collision
0x54E6256 values of 255·sin(atan(p))
0x55E6256 values of 255·cos(atan(p))
0x56E664 values of atan, in 1/256 of a turn

The one at 0x539C is read twice, with the index and with its complement to 0x40, and out come the sine and the cosine from a single table. And when the final angle is assembled, the two mirror bits are combined using parity: an and a over 0xE1A2 and a jp pe, because negating the angle is needed when exactly one of the two axes is mirrored.

A twelve-byte square root

0x5376 takes the root of HL by subtracting odd numbers: 1, then 0, then 2 and 2, then 4 and 4… and returns how many rounds it survived. Run over all 65,536 possible values, it returns exactly round(sqrt(n)), the root rounded to the nearest integer rather than truncated.

It is used for the distance between the cue ball and the aiming point, which is what divides the two shot components to leave them as a unit vector. Half a pixel there shows up in the direction.

The sound player

Three nine-byte channels from 0xE010, each with its counter, its pointer into the score and the sound number it is playing, which doubles as its priority: a new sound only gets in if its number is greater than or equal to the one playing.

What you request is not a channel but a sound, and depending on its number one, two or three channels start at once with consecutive pointers from the table at 0x5D1C. Below 0x87 it is a one-channel effect; from 0x8A up, three-channel music.

Notes carry the duration in the high nibble and the semitone in the low one, and semitone 12 is a rest. The frequency divisor comes from the table of twelve at 0x5D08 and is doubled once per octave to be dropped. The volume decays on its own, in software, by comparing two counters.

And a detail of the kind that pleases: the channel's volume register comes from rotating C — which holds 1, 3 or 5 — and adding 0x88. That gives 8, 9 and 10, which are exactly the PSG's three volume registers.

Things that are there and do nothing

  • 0x5962 writes (ix+002h) twice in a row with the same value. Three bytes that change nothing.
  • At 0x4462 there are four bytes (00 80 40 C0) that no instruction references, and the byte sequence of their address does not occur anywhere in the ROM. The menu code jumps over them. They look like an abandoned table.
  • Sound pointer number 0 does not exist: its two bytes are durations 44 and 84 from the table above, spliced into this one. Nobody asks for sound 0.

And two that do, and are the best of it

push hl followed by pop hl turns up several times in the routines that write sprites and cards (0x59E8, 0x5A01, 0x5B66). They move nothing: they are twenty-one clock cycles of waiting so the VDP has time between two consecutive writes.

And at 0x5488 there is an ld a,r. The R register is the dynamic memory refresh counter, which changes on its own, so it serves as a random number for free. The cartridge keeps its low bit and uses it to flip bit 0 of the slope it has just computed: every direction comes out with one unit of noise on it.
